<TextTwoColsNRows 
  content="<b>[2] Illusion 1: Phantom Tactile Sensation</b>
  When two vibrators, which are placed closely on the skin, are simultaneously actuated, an illusory vibration will be perceived. When their vibration intensities are the same, the phantom vibration will be generated in the midpoint of these two vibrators.
  
  <b>[3] Illusion 2: Tactile Apparent Motion</b>
  When two actuators are placed on the skin at a certain distance and vibrate in sequence with overlapped vibration time, a movement across the skin between the actuators will be perceived (SOA: Inter-stimulus Onset Asynchrony)."

<TextTwoColsNRows 
  content="<b>1. Intro</b>
  After the participant gave informed consent, she/he was introduced to the study about its setup and cautions.
  
  <b>2. Preparations</b>
  After the introduction, participants were asked to wear a pair of noise-canceling headphones and sit naturally on the chair with the pre-attached vibration motors. The height of the chair’s backrest was adjusted so that the center motor was at the same height as the participant’s navel. She/he was requested to adjust the posture so her/his back was fully touching the chairs’ backrest.

  <b>3. Pre-test</b>
  A pre-test was performed first. Each of the nine motors was activated one by one randomly. After each vibration, the participant was asked if she/he could feel a single vibration without feeling uncomfortable. This step aimed to ensure that the hardware was functioning properly and that the participants were able to perceive all motors.

  <b>4. Session 1: Localization</b>
  Pairs of motors vibrated simultaneously. There were 8 motor pairs to be tested. The participant spoke out the perceived vibration position on the sheet of motor grid for each motor pair in random order. This was repeated 5 times each in a different random order (blocked random). Each of them performed 5 × 8 = 40 trials. A short break was given after this session.

  <b>5. Session 2: Direction Perception</b>
  As mentioned earlier, this session consisted of 40 unique vibration sequences. These 40 sequences were presented twice in blocked random order. There was a short break between two blocks. In this session, she/he was shown the sheet with numbered arrows and  requested to speak out the number of the perceived direction indicated by the arrow.

  <b>6. Debriefing and Ending</b>
  At the end of the experiment, participants were asked if they have any other questions or observations about the experiment to share. Then, they were debriefed and transferred the compensation."

<SemiTitle
  toc="Take-away" 
  title="7. Take-away" 
/>

<TextTwoColsNRows 
  content="<b>Dispersion of localization of phantom vibration</b>
  In each motor-pair condition during the localization session, participants’ responses were noticeably scattered across the area of the motor grid. One possible explanation is that participants faced challenges in distinguishing the related locations between motors in the back. Firstly, participants did not know the boundaries of the vibration or the size of the dot board beforehand. Secondly, even though the illustration included an identification of the distance between the rightmost of the dot board and the spine, it is difficult for the participants to judge how long that distance is on their backs. Another potential reason is that the vibrations may have been spread out to an extent that made it challenging to pinpoint the exact source location. A motor’s vibration covers a certain area of the skin, which could be large enough so that the participant would fail to locate the center vibration point.
  
  <b>Higher correct recognition rate in diagonal</b>
  As for the direction condition, the correct identification of the direction in different center-vibration conditions happens more often when the directions are diagonal. Two possible reasons account for the different effectiveness of Tactile Apparent Motion on diagonal and non-diagonal directions. One is the variation of distance between subsequent vibrations.  In the present study’s experiment, this distance was larger when the direction was diagonal due to the three-by-three motor matrix, which could make perceiving the direction easier. It is possible that in the vertical and horizontal direction conditions, the vibrations were too close and that the participants had difficulties distinguishing the vibrations. judge how long that distance is on their backs. The other is related to the aforementioned problem of locating the vibration, which results in participants’ confusion about the relative locations of sequentially activated vibrations, and further caused a negative impact on their direction judgment.
  
  <b>Possibility to use phantom vibration in apparent motion</b>
  In some situations, substituting the vibration from actual motors with phantom vibrations from two simultaneously vibrated motors leads the participants to perceive the direction correctly. This validated some previous trials of researchers who applied the same technique aiming at simplifying the haptic devices (e.g., Israr & Poupyrev, 2011)."
