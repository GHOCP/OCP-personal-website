<TextTwoColsNRows 
  content="This study explored the relationship between daily light exposure and psychological states, specifically mood fluctuations and individual stress levels. With a focus on the interplay of light and mental well-being, the research drew from existing literature to form hypotheses and employed a longitudinal quantitative design involving participants who were TU/e students. The multilevel analyses revealed that increased illuminance was associated with reduced stress levels but corresponded to lower mood ratings. Substantial variability in individual responses across participants and days highlighted the influence of personal characteristics. Despite limitations such as the absence of contextual information these findings suggested a nuanced relationship between the three factors."

The interests of this study lay on two factors that are part of the psychological states: mood and stress level. <SemiTitle 
  toc="Procedure" 
  title="3. The Procedure" 
/>

<TextTwoColsNRows 
  content="<b>1-Beginning</b>
  PParticipants received a light logger and instructions one to two days before the seven-day measurement period. They were told to wear the light logger on their collar, close to their chin during waking hours. They placed it nearby during activities like showering. At night, the light logger was to be placed near the bed face down. Participants downloaded the M-Path app, acknowledged privacy terms, and added the study to receive notifications.
  
  <b>2-The 1st Day</b>
  On the first measurement day, participants completed a five to ten-minute intake questionnaire. Daily, participants responded to ten one-minute questionnaires as prompted by M-Path between 8:00 am and 11:00 pm.
  
  <b>3-A Seven-day Study</b>
  Each morning, participants were prompted to fill out a three-minute sleep diary. After the seven-day period, participants returned the light logger and ended the study involvement.
  
  <b>4-Data Analysis</b>
  Data analysis was executed through the Stata program. Following data cleaning, preparation, and analysis of descriptive statistics, a pairwise correlation was performed to assess connections between illuminance, mood, and stress, as well as general descriptive statistics for determining interactions and assessing the appropriateness of further analysis tools. Furthermore, two multilevel analyses were conducted with stress and mood as dependent variables and illuminance as independent variables on participant and day level."

<TextTwoColsNRows 
  content="<b>Multilevel Model of Stress and Illuminance</b>
  Since the data of Illuminance and Stress were measured with multiple days nested within participants, a 3-level model was conducted with Stress as the dependent variable and Illuminace as the independent variable. The model had random intercepts at both the participant and the day-within-participants levels. The results shown in Table 2 demonstrated that an increase in illuminance was associated with decreased stress levels (𝛽=-1.807,  p < 0.05). The model revealed considerable variability in stress, with significant differences between participants (σ2= 31.527) and notable day-to-day fluctuations (σ2= 50.222). A likelihood ratio test affirmed that incorporating a third level for days markedly enhanced the model's fit (ꭓ2= 50.8, p < 0.0001), compared to a model only accounting for individual differences.
  
  <b>Multilevel Model of Stress and Illuminance</b>
  Likewise, the relationship between Mood and Illuminance was revealed in a 3-level model with the same structure except that Mood acts as the dependent variable. The results are shown in Table 2. The model suggested that increased illuminance correlated with decreased happiness(𝛽=-1.642,  p < 0.05). Notably, there was considerable variability in happiness between participants (σ2= 57.166) and across days (σ2=  81.449), indicating that mood fluctuated significantly at the individual level and daily. A likelihood ratio test confirmed the model's enhanced fit: the three-level model significantly outperforms the two-level model (ꭓ2= 190.17, p < 0.0001)."

<SemiTitle 
  toc="Conclusion" 
  title="5. Conclusion" 
/>

<TextTwoColsNRows 
  content="<b>Big variability across participants and days</b>
  The results revealed that substantial variability in stress and mood responses could be observed across participants and days, underlying the influence of personal characteristics on daily fluctuations beyond illuminance.
  
  <b>More light made lower mood ratings</b>
  However, the three-level Mood-Illuminance model demonstrated that higher light exposure levels resulted in lower mood ratings. This incongruency with some previous studies (Beute & Kort, 2018; Figueiro et al., 2019) which stated a positive correlation between light intensity and mood ratings is worth further exploration.
  
  <b>Increased light exposure led to reduced stress</b>
  The three-level Stress-Illuminance model indicated that increased light exposure was associated with reduced stress. This was consistent with some previous studies (Chang Li et al., 2020; Beute & Kort, 2018) regarding the direction of the effect of illuminance on stress levels."

<TextTwoColsNRows 
  content="Yet the validity of these results could be affected by notable limitations in this study. First, the contextual information accompanying participants' responses was absent. This made it challenging to understand the surrounding circumstances of participants’ psychological states. Second,  the study was conducted during winter with frequent rain, during which participants had to cover light loggers, hampering the reliability of the illuminance data. Moreover, no mechanism was in place to assess whether participants consistently used the light logger. Additionally, there was a low response rate to the surveys, which resulted in gaps in the analysis, influencing the overall comprehensiveness of our findings. Another constraint arises from the lack of control for contextual and personal factors such as chronotype due to a lack of responses from participants, as well as demographic data due to not being asked."
